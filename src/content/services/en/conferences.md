## What changes compared with the portable system

Here the equipment is not ours, and we say so in the first line.

A conference in an auditorium with a stage and a PA calls for an acoustic
booth, a sound desk, an operating technician and receivers in quantity —
usually hundreds. None of that fits in a case. We hire that package from
suppliers we already work with, and it appears in the quote itemised rather
than folded into a single number.

## What stays ours

- **The coordination.** One point of contact, from briefing to close.
- **The English–Portuguese booth.** The two of us, not subcontracted.
- **Staffing the other booths.** When the event has Spanish, French or another
  language, we assemble the team from conference interpreters we already know,
  and we answer for their quality.
- **The preparation material.** Glossaries, speaker decks and briefing with the
  organisers beforehand.

## Why it is always two interpreters per booth

This is not an upsell, it is standard practice in the profession, and it exists
for a mechanical reason: simultaneous interpretation consumes sustained
attention at a level that cannot be held alone for more than twenty to thirty
minutes without a measurable drop in quality. The pair rotates in short shifts,
and each supports the other with numbers, names and terminology.

A booth staffed by one interpreter for a full day is an event that will get
worse after lunch, noticeably so to the people listening.

## Sizes we cover

From a hundred-seat auditorium with a single language pair up to conferences
running several booths at once. Above our own equipment, scale is a question of
hiring and lead time — what we need to know early is the date, the room format
and how many languages.
