## The platforms

We work on the four that come up most often in agency briefings:

- **Zoom** — native interpretation, with language channels inside the meeting
  itself
- **KUDO**
- **Interprefy**
- **Interactio**

If your event runs on something else, it can almost always be accommodated.
Worth asking.

## What we need from you

Remote interpreting is more sensitive to audio than on-site work, because the
interpreter cannot look at the room and infer what was said.

1. **Clean audio from the speakers.** A desk mic, a headset or a lapel mic. Not
   the laptop's built-in microphone.
2. **Speaker discipline.** One person at a time, microphones muted when not
   speaking.
3. **A technical rehearsal.** Twenty minutes, a few days ahead, with the people
   who will actually be speaking. It is the difference between an uneventful
   event and one with an apology in the middle of it.

## The most common failure, and why it matters

The number one problem in remote events is a speaker using a laptop
microphone in a room with hard surfaces.

What happens: the microphone picks up the voice reflected off the walls
alongside the direct voice. To people in the meeting it sounds “muffled, but
followable” — the listening brain compensates. For an interpreter, who has to
resolve every word in real time and render it in another language, that
compensation is not available. Intelligibility collapses far earlier for the
person interpreting than for the person merely listening.

The result is slower interpretation with more gaps, and nobody can identify the
cause, because the audio “sounded fine”. It was fine for listeners, not for
interpreters.

A cheap headset on the speaker fixes it. It is the least expensive
recommendation we have.
